The heater is the heart of any sauna. It decides how fast the room warms up, how evenly the heat spreads and how soft the steam feels. The short answer: **choose a wood-burning heater if your sauna is outdoors, a chimney is possible and you want a real fire; choose an electric heater if you value convenience, precise temperature and no chimney.** Size it by room volume — the general rule of thumb is roughly 1 kW per 1 m³.

Below we compare both types, show how to size the heater, explain why stones matter, what control panels do and which safety rules you should never skip.

## Wood-burning vs electric sauna heater: the key differences

| Criterion | Wood-burning heater | Electric heater |
|---|---|---|
| Atmosphere | Live fire, scent of wood, traditional feel | Quiet, clean, even heat |
| Heating up | Stack wood, light it, tend the fire | Press a button, the thermostat does the rest |
| Temperature control | Approximate — by the amount of wood and draft | Precise — thermostat and timer |
| Installation needs | Chimney, fire-safe roof/ceiling pass-through, wood storage | Dedicated circuit and breaker, sometimes three-phase power |
| Best for | Country houses, gardens, out-of-town plots | Indoor saunas, apartments, hotels, spas |
| Maintenance | Ash removal, chimney checks | Checking stones and the sensor |

### Who should choose a wood-burning heater?

- Anyone building a sauna **in the garden, at a country house or outside the city** who wants a classic bathhouse atmosphere.
- Locations where the electrical supply is weak or can't handle a powerful heater.
- People who enjoy the ritual — splitting wood, lighting the fire, listening to it crackle.

Wood-burning heaters are especially popular in outdoor saunas: the chimney runs neatly through the roof, and the stove gives strong, "deep" heat even in a larger room.

### Who should choose an electric heater?

- **Indoor saunas** — in a basement, next to a bathroom, inside a villa. No chimney is needed, so electric is the default choice for indoor builds.
- People who use the sauna often, for example for an hour after work.
- Hotels and spas, where stable temperature and timer control matter.

## Sizing: heater kW vs room volume (m³)

The most common mistake is choosing heater output by guesswork. The calculation is simple:

1. **Find the room volume:** width × length × height. Example: 2 m × 2 m × 2.1 m = 8.4 m³.
2. **Rule of thumb:** for a well-insulated room, about 1 kW per 1 m³.
3. **Adjust for heat-hungry surfaces:** each 1 m² of uninsulated surface — glass, tile, stone — adds roughly 1.2 m³ to the calculated volume. In a cabin sauna with a panoramic glass front, this makes a real difference.
4. **Check the manufacturer's table:** every heater's manual states the room volume range it's designed for. Make the final call based on that range.

| Room volume (approx.) | Suggested output (rule of thumb) |
|---|---|
| 4–6 m³ | 4.5–6 kW |
| 6–9 m³ | 6–9 kW |
| 9–13 m³ | 9–12 kW |
| 13 m³ and above | 12 kW and up, sometimes two heaters |

An **undersized heater** takes ages to warm the room and may never reach the target temperature. An **oversized one** cycles on and off constantly and makes the air dry and harsh. A correctly sized heater usually brings the room to a comfortable temperature in roughly 40–60 minutes, depending on insulation and outdoor weather.

## Sauna stones: why they matter

Stones store the heater's energy and release it gently into the room. The steam you get when you throw water on them — the Finns call it *löyly* — depends directly on stone quality.

- Use **volcanic stones made specifically for saunas**, such as olivine diabase or peridotite. They tolerate constant heating and cooling cycles.
- **Don't use river stones or random rocks** — they can hold moisture inside and crack when heated.
- Stack stones **loosely, not tightly**, so air can flow between them. In an electric heater the elements must be fully covered but never wedged in.
- Check the stones at least once a year and replace any that have crumbled or cracked.

## Control panels: what to know

Electric heaters come with two types of control:

- **Built-in controls** — buttons or dials on the heater itself. Simple and reliable for small saunas.
- **Separate control panel** — mounted on the wall outside the sauna room. You set temperature, timer and sometimes lighting from one place. Some models allow pre-set start times or app control.

**Electrical requirements:** larger heaters typically need a three-phase supply (380–400 V). Cable size, breaker and earthing must be checked by a qualified electrician.

## Safety rules

1. **Respect clearances.** The manufacturer specifies minimum distances from the heater to wooden walls, ceiling and benches.
2. **Install a heater guard** — a wooden rail around the stove prevents accidental burns.
3. **Mount the temperature sensor** of an electric heater exactly as the manual says; the wrong spot can cause overheating.
4. **A wood-burning chimney** must be insulated, with a fire-safe pass-through at the ceiling and roof. Clean it regularly.
5. **Ventilation** with fresh-air intake and exhaust matters for comfort and safety.
6. **Never dry towels or clothes on the heater.**

## Which heater is right for you? A quick checklist

- Sauna inside the house → **electric heater**.
- Sauna in the garden, chimney possible, you want a real fire → **wood-burning heater**.
- Weak electrical supply → **wood-burning heater**, or upgrade the supply.
- Daily use, hotel or spa → **electric heater with a separate control panel**.
- Cabin sauna with a panoramic glass front → size the heater **with the glass area in mind**.

## FAQ

### How do I calculate the right sauna heater size?

Work out the room volume (width × length × height) and allow about 1 kW per 1 m³. For every 1 m² of uninsulated surface such as glass, stone or tile, add about 1.2 m³ to the volume. Then confirm with the manufacturer's sizing table.

### Is a wood-burning or electric sauna heater better?

There's no universal answer. Wood-burning gives a traditional atmosphere and independence from the grid; electric gives convenience, precise temperature and no chimney. Indoor saunas usually go electric, while outdoor saunas work well with either.

### How often should sauna stones be replaced?

Inspect and restack them at least once a year, more often with heavy use. Replace crumbled or cracked stones — they block airflow and can shorten the heater's life.

### Does an electric sauna heater need three-phase power?

Some lower-output heaters run on single-phase, but larger models usually require three-phase. The heater's manual and a qualified electrician should decide.

### Can I swap the heater later?

Yes, as long as the new heater fits the room in size, output and safety clearances. Switching from wood-burning to electric may require preparing a new electrical circuit.
